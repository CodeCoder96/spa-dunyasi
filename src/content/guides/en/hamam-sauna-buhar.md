## Three heat rooms, three business models

Developers and spa operators often ask us the same question at the start of a project: should we build a hammam, a sauna or a steam room? From a corridor they can look alike, simply warm rooms behind a door. In practice they differ in how guests use them, how much space they take, what they are built from and how they are run day to day.

This guide deliberately leaves aside what each experience does to the body. It compares the three purely as an operator would: the character of the space, the footprint, the materials, the building services and the staffing. That is usually enough to narrow the choice, and often to show that a combination works better than any single option.

## The hammam: a ritual with a team behind it

The hammam comes from the Turkish bath tradition and is a sequence of spaces rather than a single room. In a classic layout, guests move from a cool entrance and resting area, through a warm intermediate room, into the hot room. At the centre of the hot room sits a large heated marble platform known as the göbek taşı, with marble basins, or kurnas, around the walls. The ceiling is typically domed, with small openings that let daylight filter down.

**What it means for operations.** A hammam is more than a space guests use on their own. It is where staff deliver services such as the kese scrub and foam wash, so a hammam project is also a staffing and menu decision. You will need trained attendants and a schedule that matches the hours the hammam is open.

**Materials.** Marble defines the space: floors, the central platform and the basins are usually marble, with marble or waterproof finishes on the walls. Slip-resistant surface treatment and a daily cleaning routine for the stone should be costed from the outset.

**Footprint.** Of the three, the hammam generally needs the most floor area. Even a compact boutique version needs generous walking space around the central platform and room for the basins.

## The sauna: a dry timber cabin

The sauna has Nordic roots. It is a timber-lined cabin with a heater topped with stones; the air inside is dry. Benches are normally arranged in tiers, and guests choose which level to sit on.

**What it means for operations.** Guests use a sauna independently, so it does not tie up staff. That is one reason saunas are so common in hotels, gyms and private homes. A glazed door makes it easy for the team to keep an eye on the room from outside.

**Materials.** The lining and benches are timber. The species you choose shapes the look, the feel of the surfaces and the cleaning approach; timber should be cleaned with suitable methods rather than flooded with water and chemicals.

**Footprint.** The sauna is the most compact of the three. It can be planned into a hotel suite, a villa basement or a gym changing room, which makes it a frequent choice for both commercial and residential projects.

## The steam room: humidity and waterproof surfaces

A steam room is a sealed space filled with steam from a generator housed in a separate plant area. Compared with a sauna, the air is humid and visibility softens as the steam builds.

**What it means for operations.** Like a sauna, it is mostly self-service. The extra workload sits with maintenance: the generator, water treatment and drainage need regular checks.

**Materials.** Every surface is in constant contact with moisture, so walls, ceiling and benches must be fully waterproof. Ceramic tiles, glass mosaic and purpose-made waterproof panels are the usual choices. A sloped ceiling lets condensation run down the walls instead of dripping on guests, and a floor drain is essential.

**Footprint.** The room itself can be as compact as a sauna, but you must also allow a plant room or cupboard for the generator.

## Side by side

A quick comparison on the points that matter most when budgeting:

- **Atmosphere.** Hammam: heated marble and hot water. Sauna: dry heat. Steam room: dense humidity.
- **Staffing.** The hammam needs attendants for its services; sauna and steam room are largely self-service.
- **Space.** The hammam takes the most room; sauna and steam room can be compact.
- **Materials.** Marble for the hammam, timber for the sauna, tiles and mosaic for the steam room.
- **Services.** Hammam and steam room depend on strong water and drainage infrastructure; for a sauna, power and ventilation come first.
- **Upkeep.** Marble, timber and tiles each need their own cleaning method, so plan staff training accordingly.

Many properties install two or all three. When they do, a shared shower zone and a shared rest area make much better use of the floor plate.

## Matching the choice to your property

**City and business hotels.** Where space is tight, a sauna and steam room pair is common. Guests use them independently while the spa team concentrates on treatment rooms.

**Resorts.** Properties with more room, and guests who visit the spa several times during a stay, often place the hammam at the heart of the offer. A Vichy shower room beside it broadens the wet-area menu.

**Day spas and beauty centres.** Let the treatment menu decide. A centre that wants to offer kese and foam rituals may justify a small hammam; one that wants to keep wet-area investment modest can start with a compact sauna or steam room.

**Gyms and residential developments.** Unstaffed use is the priority, so sauna and steam room lead. A swimsuit dryer in the changing room is a practical addition for members using the pool or wet area.

**Private homes.** Villas and detached houses most often choose a sauna. Where space and plumbing allow, a small hammam or steam room is also possible.

## Daily running and upkeep

The routines your team repeats every day should also influence the decision.

In a **hammam**, the central platform, basins and floors are cleaned daily, and textiles such as peshtemals, scrub mitts and foam bags need a laundry and drying flow. Attendant rotas are built around opening hours.

In a **sauna**, benches and floor are wiped down, the heater stones are checked periodically and ventilation is kept clear. Asking guests to sit on a towel helps keep the timber clean.

In a **steam room**, grout lines, the drain grate and the steam outlet are inspected regularly. Leaving the door open outside opening hours to air the room is a good habit.

Writing these routines down before opening helps new staff maintain the same standard.

## What to prepare before the first meeting

Whichever room you are leaning towards, these details speed up the process:

1. **Dimensions.** Length, width and ceiling height of the space, and a floor plan if you have one.
2. **Site conditions.** Floor levels, existing water and drainage runs, the position of the electrical panel and ventilation options.
3. **Intended use.** Hotel, spa, gym or home; self-service use or staffed services.
4. **Design direction.** A traditional marble look, or a cleaner timber-led style.
